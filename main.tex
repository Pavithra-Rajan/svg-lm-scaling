\section{Introduction}

Scaling laws for neural language models have emerged as one of the most practically useful empirical findings in modern deep learning. Kaplan et al.\cite{1} demonstrated that model loss follows a predictable power-law as a function of model size, dataset size, and compute budget, enabling practitioners to extrapolate the performance of large, expensive models from small, cheap runs. Subsequent work by Hoffmann et al.\cite{2} refined these findings into compute-optimal training recipes. However, nearly all such studies target natural language and far less is known about whether these relationships hold for structured, programmatic representations of visual content.
\vspace{5pt}

Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) occupies an interesting middle ground between code and visual art. An SVG file is a sequence of XML-tagged drawing commands like paths, shapes, gradients, and transforms, that a renderer interprets to produce an image. This structure makes SVGs to be treated as a programming language. Yet the semantics are fundamentally visual. Recent work such as StarVector~\cite{4} has demonstrated that language models can be trained to generate and understand SVG, opening the door to automated icon synthesis, vector graphic editing, and scalable design tools. However, it remains unclear how model capacity translates to generation quality in this domain. Does loss improve at the same rate as in natural language? Do the same power-law exponents hold? Can learning rates be transferred across scales without re-sweeping?
\vspace{5pt}

This project investigates scaling laws for decoder-only Transformer language models trained exclusively on SVG data. I make two primary contributions. First, I train five GPT-style models spanning roughly two orders of magnitude in parameter count, from 853,120 (Tiny) to 85,347,072 (XL), on a curated corpus of vector icon SVGs and fit power-law curves of the form $L = a \cdot N^{-\alpha} + c$ to characterize how validation loss scales with model size. Second, I evaluate Maximal Update Parameterization ($\mu$P)\cite{4} as a mechanism for learning-rate transfer. By reparameterizing the model in $\mu$P coordinates, the optimal learning rate at small scale should remain optimal at large scale, potentially eliminating the need for expensive per-scale hyperparameter sweeps.
\vspace{1pt}

The pipeline covers the full machine learning life cycle comprising of data collection and cleaning, BPE tokenizer training, binary dataset preparation, model definition, learning-rate sweeps, scaling runs, quantitative analysis, and a qualitative evaluation of generated SVG samples. Through this end-to-end study, the aim is to characterize whether the favorable scaling properties observed in natural language carry over to the SVG domain, and whether $\mu$P provides a reliable shortcut for hyperparameter transfer in this setting.
\\

\section{Data}

\subsection{Dataset Description and Preprocessing Pipeline}

The training corpus is assembled from four publicly released collections in the StarVector \cite{4} family of SVG datasets, all distributed via Hugging Face: \texttt{svg-icons-simple} (primary), \texttt{svg-emoji-simple}, \texttt{svg-stack-simple}, and \texttt{svg-fonts-simple}. The first two are loaded in full, while the latter two are streamed and truncated once an estimated 100M-token training budget is met. The icons set provides clean, monochromatic vector glyphs, while the emoji set adds color, gradients, and small decorative motifs; \texttt{svg-stack} contributes diversity from in-the-wild assets such as logos and UI components; and \texttt{svg-fonts} contributes typographic glyph paths. This blend keeps the corpus dominated by icon-style SVGs while exposing the model to a wider stylistic distribution.
\vspace{4pt}

The preprocessing pipeline is intentionally aggressive, since SVGs in the real-world contain large amounts of vendor-specific markup, embedded comments, and high-precision coordinates that inflate the vocabulary without aiding generation. Each file is passed through an \texttt{lxml} parser that strips comments, processing instructions, and DOM subtrees rooted at \texttt{<metadata>}, \texttt{<title>}, \texttt{<desc>}, and \texttt{<defs>}. Empty namespace declarations are then collapsed via \texttt{etree.cleanup\_namespaces}, all numeric literals are rounded to one decimal place (e.g.\ \texttt{12.345678} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{12.3}), and runs of whitespace are normalized to a single space. SVGs that fail to parse, or whose cleaned form falls outside the $[50,\,8192]$-character window, are discarded. The lower bound rejects degenerate stubs (empty paths, single rects) and the upper bound matches roughly the 512-token context window used in training, after the BPE compression ratio is taken into account.
\vspace{4pt}

Cleaned SVGs are written to a single \texttt{all\_svgs.jsonl} artifact, which serves as input to both tokenizer training and the binary packing stage. The corpus is then tokenized end-to-end, every sequence whose token length falls outside $[4,\,513]$ is discarded, and the remaining sequences are deterministically shuffled with seed 42 and split 98/1/1 into train, validation, and test partitions \emph{at the SVG level} so that no document straddles a split boundary. Each partition is concatenated into a flat \texttt{uint16} NumPy array (\texttt{train.npy}, \texttt{val.npy}, \texttt{test.npy}) following the nanoGPT~\cite{5} layout, and the dataloader samples random contiguous windows of length \texttt{block\_size}=512 from these arrays. As every document is wrapped with \texttt{<bos>}~\dots~\texttt{<eos>}, the model receives a clear in-band signal of document boundaries even when a sampled window straddles two SVGs.

\subsection{Tokenization Scheme}

Tokenization uses Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) with a 4{,}096-token vocabulary, a byte-level pre-tokenizer (so any UTF-8 character can be represented and \texttt{<unk>} is essentially never emitted), and a \texttt{TemplateProcessing} post-processor that wraps every encoded sequence with the special tokens \texttt{<bos>}~\dots~\texttt{<eos>}. The vocabulary also reserves \texttt{<pad>} and \texttt{<unk>}. Their integer IDs are persisted in \texttt{token\_config.json} for use by the dataloader and generator. A 4{,}096-entry vocabulary sits between the 1{,}024-token character-level extreme and the 8{,}192-token wordpiece end of the spectrum. It is large enough to capture the most common path-command motifs, but small enough that the embedding matrix remains a small fraction of total parameters at every model scale.
\vspace{4pt}

The merges learned by the trainer reveal the structure of the SVG ``language''. The very first merges fuse the leading space character (encoded as \texttt{G} in byte-level BPE) with single digits and with the path commands \texttt{L}, \texttt{C}, and \texttt{M}, reflecting how SVG path data is dominated by sequences of the form \texttt{L\,x\,y} or \texttt{C\,x\textsubscript{1}\,y\textsubscript{1}\,x\textsubscript{2}\,y\textsubscript{2}\,x\,y}. Shortly after, full attribute keywords emerge as single tokens (\texttt{Gstroke}, \texttt{Gfill}, \texttt{Gwidth}), followed by frequent integer prefixes (\texttt{G12}, \texttt{G13}, \texttt{G14}, \dots), and finally rarer multi-digit coordinates seen in the larger viewboxes of \texttt{svg-stack} (\texttt{G2343}, \texttt{1928}). As an example, the below icon

\section{Results}

This section reports the outcomes from section 3 detailing the learning-rate sweep (with the SP--$\mu$P LR-transfer comparison), the full one-epoch scaling runs and power-law fit, the extrapolation to $10\times$XL, and finally the training-loss plots and a qualitative evaluation of generated samples from the best checkpoint. All numbers are mean validation cross-entropy in nats per token. A ``nat" (natural unit of information) is a unit of information entropy based on base-e logarithms.

Each of the seven LR candidates (Table~\ref{tab:lr_grid}) was trained on Tiny for 400 steps under both parameterizations. \end{table}

The negative $\alpha$ means the fit, taken at face value, predicts loss to \emph{grow} with $N$ over the measured range. Mechanically this reflects the two divergent SP runs dominating the regression rather than a property of SVG language modeling itself. The three stable SP points (Tiny, Small, Medium) on their own give a positive scaling slope. Extrapolating the SP fit to a hypothetical $10\times$XL model ($\sim$916M params) yields a predicted loss of \emph{$2.51$ nats} (Table~\ref{tab:extrap}), but this number is not meaningfully predictive. It merely says that, if a fixed-LR SP run at $10\times$XL behaved like the $1\times$XL run, the loss would worsen further. A more honest projection would re-fit on the $\mu$P points (which scale monotonically from $0.73$ to $0.69$ over a $48\times$ widening) and predict a $\mu$P $10\times$XL loss in the low-$0.6$s. I report the SP extrapolation here for completeness, with the caveat that the divergence at Large/XL violates the power-law assumption. 

\subsection{Training Curves}

Figure~\ref{fig:train_curves} shows training and validation loss versus optimizer step for the five SP runs. Tiny/Small/Medium descend smoothly toward the $0.6$--$0.7$ plateau, while Large and XL show high-variance, plateau-then-spike trajectories typical of LR-induced instability at large width. The instability is visible by step $\sim$500 in the XL curve and never recovers across the remaining $\sim$1{,}700 steps. This is consistent with the scaling-table picture. It is not that Large/XL are under-trained, but that the optimizer is operating outside its stable regime under SP at this width.

\section{Discussion}


\subsection{Key Insights from the Scaling Experiments}

The headline takeaway is that ``bigger is better'' does not hold automatically for this corpus. Under Standard Parameterization with a single Tiny-tuned learning rate, the best validation loss came from \texttt{sp\_medium} at $10.8$M parameters, both larger sizes (Large, XL) ended up worse than every smaller size. So the SP curve is not a clean power law but a U-shape with a sweet spot in the middle. This was an unexpected result. However, it reframes the scaling question from ``how big should the model be?'' to ``can we train large widths stably at all?''. Once we control for stability via $\mu$P, validation loss does fall as $N$ grows, but only mildly. The $\mu$P column compresses into a $0.65$--$0.73$ band over a $48\times$ widening, which is closer to ``returns are starting to flatten'' than to the Kaplan-style $\alpha \approx 0.076$ slope seen in natural language.

\subsection{Interpreting the LR Sweep and the $\mu$P Result}

The two LR sweeps both selected $6{\times}10^{-3}$ on Tiny, but that coincidence is not the LR-transfer property. The actual claim that the small-model optimum stays optimal at large width is what fails for SP and holds for $\mu$P in our data. SP at $6{\times}10^{-3}$ runs cleanly through Medium and then breaks at Large and XL, with training curves showing the plateau-and-spike signature of an LR that is just outside the stable regime. $\mu$P at the same nominal $6{\times}10^{-3}$ stays intact across all five sizes. The headline is therefore not that $\mu$P beats SP on absolute loss. It indicates that $\mu$P removes the per-size LR sweep that SP would otherwise need to make Large and XL trainable. 

\subsection{Design Decisions and Their Impact}

Three early choices shaped what the rest of the project could and could not measure. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The aggressive preprocessing like rounding numerics to one decimal place, stripping metadata, capping length pushed the corpus into the 256--512 token band where a 512-context model can see whole documents, which is what made document-level loss meaningful. 
    \item The 4{,}096-token byte-level BPE vocabulary kept the embedding matrix small enough that even XL is dominated by transformer parameters rather than the embedding, which is what makes the scaling axis interpretable. 
    \item The decision to use SP weight tying but drop it in $\mu$P (purely for implementation simplicity) created a $2 V d_{\text{model}}$ accounting gap in raw parameter counts that had to be normalized before comparing the two curves.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Three classes of limitations are worth naming. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compute and data}: the single-epoch budget puts XL at $\sim$1.7 training tokens per parameter, far below the $\sim$20 tokens/param Chinchilla recommends, so the $\mu$P plateau may be a data-bottleneck rather than a model-capacity result. A natural next step is multi-epoch training on a $4$--$10\times$ larger token budget, which would also refit the power law with a credible irreducible-loss term $c$.
    \item \textbf{Method coverage}: $\mu$P base shapes here vary only along the width axis, so depth scaling and head-count scaling were never tested as transferable hyperparameters. A fuller study would sweep depth too. 
    \item \textbf{Generation}: prefix-conditioned completion failed in three of the five attempted prompts because the model was trained purely on full documents wrapped in \texttt{<bos>}\,$\dots$\,\texttt{<eos>}, with no fill-in-the-middle objective, and there is no conditioning signal (captions, classes) that would let the model be \emph{asked} to draw a particular concept. Adding (i) training on randomly-sliced documents, (ii) a small image-conditioned StarVector-style head, and (iii) a held-out human-rated quality benchmark would convert this from a scaling-study artifact into a usable SVG generator. The current work establishes the scaling skeleton.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusion}
This work investigates scaling behavior for decoder-only Transformers trained exclusively on Scalable Vector Graphics, fitting five GPT-style architectures from $853$K to $85.3$M parameters on a $145.6$M-token corpus. Under Standard Parameterization with a fixed learning rate of $6{\times}10^{-3}$, validation loss decreases from Tiny to Medium and then diverges at Large and XL, a non-monotonic curve attributable to large-width training instability rather than to scaling itself. 
\\

Under Maximal Update Parameterization, the same learning rate produces stable scaling across all five sizes, confirming $\mu$P's learning-rate-transfer property for this domain. The best checkpoint by validation loss is \texttt{sp\_medium} ($10.8$M parameters, $0.6322$ nats/token, test perplexity $1.87$). Unconditional samples from this checkpoint are $98\%$ XML-valid, $98\%$ renderable, and consistently include a \texttt{viewBox}, indicating that the model has internalized SVG surface syntax. Prefix-conditioned generation fails for most, attributable to a training-objective mismatch rather than to capacity limits. 
\\

The single-epoch set-up leaves XL well below the Chinchilla compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratio, so reported losses are upper bounds. This indicates that multi-epoch training, fill-in-the-middle objectives, and image-conditioned signals are natural next steps. The principal contribution is to demonstrate that $\mu$P is the practical mechanism that makes multi-scale SVG language modeling tractable on modest compute, and that further gains will come from richer training objectives rather than from increased model width alone.
